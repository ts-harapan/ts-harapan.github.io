Javan rhino recovery simulation

How many Javan rhinos are left decides what to do next. This model projects the Ujung Kulon population from two starting counts and compares full protection, habitat recovery and captive breeding, including when to start and which mitochondrial haplotypes to catch.

How fast can a female breed?

Desy, a wild female in Ujung Kulon, has had five calves since 2013, one every 3.4 years on average. That matches the 33% yearly breeding rate used in the 2016 PHVA. At the Sumatran Rhino Sanctuary in Way Kambas, the most successful mother, Ratu, had three calves in 2012–2023, one every 5.6 years. Rosa had one calf after eight pregnancy losses, and Delilah, born at the sanctuary, had her first in 2023.

Javan rhinoSumatran rhino

There is no record of Javan rhinos breeding in captivity, so the model uses the Sumatran Rhino Sanctuary (SRS) record for captive breeding. Model rates: wild ≈ 0.33 births per adult female per year; SRS-like ≈ 0.12 (5 births from 3 mothers over about 42 breeding-age female-years, 2012–2025).

Precedents

Nepal: from Chitwan to new sites

Nepal moved 104 greater one-horned rhinos out of Chitwan in 1986–2023 to Bardia, Suklaphanta and Koshi Tappu, 88 of them to Bardia. Bardia's rhinos still fell from 67 in 2000 to 22 by 2008 during a poaching surge, so more were moved in. New sites only grow when protection holds.

Santiapillai & Suprahman, 1986

With 54 rhinos, they advised waiting until the population reached about 80, and starting with free-range breeding of a few animals inside the park complex.

What "full protection" means here

In the model it means no rhinos are poached from 2027 onward. In the 2016 PHVA, adult mortality was the parameter with the largest effect on growth, and poaching was the only threat that drove modelled populations extinct. In practice that takes intensive Rhino Protection Unit patrols, camera-trap monitoring that would detect missing animals, secure handling of monitoring data, and support from villages around the park.

Haplotypes and sex ratio

Mitochondrial DNA passes only from mother to calf, so a haplotype survives only through its females. If haplotype 2 is rare, losing a few of its females can erase it. The model follows each female line, so you can see whether a strategy keeps haplotype 2. Video-trap records from Ujung Kulon show a slightly male-biased population: 36 males and 32 females among 68 identified rhinos in 2019; among adults, 28 males to 23 females, about 1.2 males per female (Putra et al. 2020). The default population is therefore 53% male. With fewer females, fewer calves are born each year, and haplotype 2 rests on even fewer mothers.

Model and limits

Parameters follow the 2016 Javan rhino PHVA: first breeding at 7 years, last at 35, 33% of adult females breeding each year (falling to 16% near capacity), mortality of 15% for calves, 2% for ages 1–6 and 4% for adults, 80% of males in the breeding pool, 20% environmental variation and inbreeding depression of 6.29 lethal equivalents (this model also assumes half of these are lethal alleles that selection can purge, the Vortex default). Run with the PHVA's own settings, it gives a mean of about 53 rhinos after 100 years (PHVA baseline mean 56). Poaching removes adults, three times more often males. Because breeding rises as numbers fall, poaching here hurts less than in the PHVA, which found 1% a year gave a 6% extinction risk and 2% a year a 68% risk over 100 years. Numbers are projections for comparing strategies, not forecasts.
